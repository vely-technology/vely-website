Most frustrating dating-app experiences come from one mismatch: two people wanted different things and neither said so. Getting clear on your own goals first, and stating them plainly, removes most of that friction before it starts. This exercise takes about twenty minutes. Do it alone if you are single, or together if you are joining an app as a couple.

## Step 1: Separate what you want from what you think you should want

Write two columns. On the left, what you would say if a friend asked what you are looking for. On the right, what you actually hope happens in the next three months. The gap between the columns is where the confusion lives. Common examples:

- "Something serious" on the left, "someone to do things with on weekends" on the right.
- "Just seeing what's out there" on the left, "a relationship, but I'm scared to say so" on the right.
- For couples: "new friends" on the left, "friends, and maybe more if it clicks" on the right.

Neither column is wrong. The right column is what belongs on your profile.

## Step 2: Choose one primary goal

Pick one of these as your main goal for now. You can change it later, but one at a time keeps your profile and your choices consistent:

1. **A committed relationship** with one person.
2. **Dating without a fixed outcome**, open to where it goes.
3. **Friendship and social life** first: people to do things with.
4. **Open-minded connections**, including ethical non-monogamy, with clear boundaries.
5. **Couple-to-couple connections**: meeting other couples together, as a pair.

If you are a couple, each partner should pick independently and then compare. If the answers differ, that conversation is the most important part of this exercise, and it needs to happen before either of you sends a like.

## Step 3: Write your non-negotiables and your flexibles

List three things that are non-negotiable (for example: lives within an hour, wants the same kind of connection, respects a slow pace) and three things you are flexible on (age range, interests, how often you meet). Most people over-filter on the flexibles and under-filter on the non-negotiables. Reverse that.

## Step 4: Put it on your profile in one sentence

Turn your primary goal into one plain sentence and make it the first line of your bio. Examples that work:

- "Looking for a relationship, not in a hurry, happy to start with coffee."
- "New in Pune, mostly here to find people to explore the city with."
- "Married couple, here to meet other couples for dinners, trips and whatever grows from genuine friendship."

Vague lines like "no drama" or "just ask" cost you matches, because nobody knows whether they fit. A clear line attracts fewer likes and far better ones.

## Step 5: Use the app's intent tools

On Vely you choose a single or couple profile, set who you want to discover (singles, couples or both) and state what you are looking for. Because chats only open after a [mutual match](/blog/how-mutual-matching-works/), that information does real work: people who do not fit simply never land in your inbox. Review your settings every few weeks; goals shift, and the app should follow.

## Step 6: Check your goal against your behaviour

After two weeks, look at who you actually liked and talked to. If your profile says "relationship" and your likes say "anyone attractive within 2 km", your actions are telling you something. Either update the goal or update the behaviour. Dating gets a lot calmer when the two agree.

## For couples: a short script

Sit down together and answer these out loud: What do we want from meeting other people? What is off the table? Who replies to messages? When do we tell a match that we are a couple (on Vely this is already on the profile)? What happens if one of us wants to stop? Ten minutes on this saves months of unspoken tension. Our guide to [dating apps for couples](/blog/dating-apps-for-couples-what-to-look-for/) covers the features that make this easier.

Clarity is not a personality trait, it is a habit. Revisit the exercise whenever dating starts to feel noisy again.
